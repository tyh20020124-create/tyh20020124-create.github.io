# Breatho — Portfolio Narrative Analysis

Status: internal narrative plan. This is not yet public webpage copy.

## What the reference portfolio is doing

Caitlin Morris does not retell a research project in the order of an academic paper. Each project is framed around one human experience. The reader first encounters a category, project name, context, and a short proposition. A design principle then explains the author’s point of view. Only after that does the text describe what the system is and what happened when people used it. Technical implementation is mentioned only when it helps the reader imagine the interaction. Publication and role information close the story rather than leading it.

For the most relevant examples, InExChange is not introduced as a pneumatic system architecture; it is introduced as “shared sensation as a medium for connection and play.” EmbER is not led by its sensors or evaluation protocol; it is framed as “designing social presence through haptic and sonic interaction.” The devices, studies, and findings then support those experiential claims. Images appear before the text and communicate the interaction faster than a methods section could.

This creates a compact sequence:

`Human experience → design principle → what the interaction feels like → how the system enables it → what people did or felt → role / publication`

## Why Breatho fits this structure

The paper presents Breatho as a real-time respiratory guidance system for amateur runners. Its strongest portfolio idea, however, is not the LRC algorithm itself. It is the transformation of breathing from an invisible internal process into a physical rhythm that can be felt while moving.

Breatho senses running cadence and converts it into gentle pneumatic pressure around the abdomen. Inflation suggests inhalation; release suggests exhalation. This bodily correspondence is more immediate than asking a runner to interpret a number, sound, or abstract vibration. The system therefore connects an external locomotor rhythm—steps—with an internal physiological rhythm—breathing.

That is the experience-level story the website should tell:

**Breatho makes an internal rhythm physically perceptible, helping runners learn breathing through the body rather than through a screen.**

## Recommended public framing

**Category**  
Embodied & Wearable Interaction

**Project title**  
Breatho

**Context**  
DIS Companion ’26 Research · National University of Singapore

**One-line proposition**  
Turning running cadence into gentle abdominal pressure, so breathing can be felt, followed, and learned in motion.

**Design principle**  
Make internal rhythms tangible without pulling attention away from the body.

This framing is strong because it communicates the project in ordinary language while remaining faithful to the paper. It also differentiates Breatho from a fitness tracker: the system does not simply measure the runner or present another metric. It creates a physical cue that participates in the act of breathing.

## The story the project page should tell

### Opening — Breathing is important, but difficult to perceive while running

The first section should begin with the runner’s experience rather than literature or physiology. Most running wearables make pace, distance, and heart rate visible, but breathing remains internal and difficult to deliberately coordinate during movement. Breatho asks whether a wearable can make that rhythm easier to feel without adding another screen or demanding constant attention.

The opening image should show the device being worn during running. A short motion clip of the belt inflating and releasing would communicate the interaction even more effectively than a static product photograph.

### Interaction — Steps become a breathing rhythm

The second section should explain the complete interaction in one clear loop. The wearable senses cadence, maps it to a breathing rhythm, and creates a gentle press-and-release cue around the abdomen. Pressure building around the body suggests inhalation; release suggests exhalation. The runner does not need to count steps or read a display because the timing is carried by the sensation itself.

This section needs one simple diagram: `steps → rhythm mapping → abdominal pressure → guided breathing`. Exact ratios, sensor models, control states, and pneumatic timings belong in the paper, not in the main portfolio narrative.

### Form — Finding where and how the body should feel the cue

The third section should show that the final wearable was not simply assembled from components. Multiple airbag configurations were tested to balance cue clarity, comfort, and freedom of movement. The research moved from a larger frontal cue toward a more compact lateral arrangement that distributed pressure more naturally across the abdomen.

This is the strongest design-process section because it links physical form to perception. An image sequence can show three configuration studies, followed by the resolved belt. The text should focus on the design decision—where the sensation should be felt and how intrusive it should be—rather than listing component specifications.

### Experience — From instruction to embodied rhythm

The fourth section should show Breatho in treadmill and outdoor running. Across iterative tests, participants were able to understand the pneumatic rhythm quickly and described the cues as stable and broadly compatible with running. The studies also suggested more regular breathing patterns and lower perceived effort when using the device.

Because the studies are preliminary and small-scale, the website should avoid presenting the project as a proven performance-enhancement product. The responsible claim is that the prototype demonstrates the feasibility of body-centred pneumatic guidance and offers early evidence that runners can follow and internalise the rhythm.

The paper’s exact RPE change and participant counts can appear in a restrained results caption if desired, but they should not become the hero claim. The stronger portfolio outcome is the relationship between cue, body, and movement.

### Reflection — A wearable should gradually disappear into the skill

The closing section should position Breatho as a learning aid rather than a permanent command system. The long-term opportunity is for guidance to become quieter as the runner internalises the rhythm. This idea gives the project a thoughtful endpoint: the best version of the system may be one that helps the user need it less over time.

The paper also identifies current limits—weight, stiffness, pump noise, motion artefacts, and limited personalisation. These should be acknowledged briefly because they explain the next design iteration. They should not be presented as a dense limitations list.

## What to keep from the paper

The portfolio should retain the research question, the cadence-to-pressure interaction, the embodied mapping between abdominal expansion and breathing, the form-factor iterations, treadmill and outdoor use, the preliminary finding of more stable breathing, perceived effort as a supporting result, and the design direction toward lighter and more personalised guidance.

## What to compress or remove

The portfolio should not reproduce the literature review, respiratory-device quadrant map, complete hardware bill of materials, microcontroller logic, detailed LRC ratio table, pump duty-cycle parameters, pressure thresholds, firmware description, questionnaire tables, every usability score, full limitations taxonomy, or numerical future engineering targets. These establish academic completeness in the paper but interrupt the experiential story online.

## Recommended image rhythm

The page can be built from six major visual moments. Open with a full-width running photograph or video. Follow with a simple cadence-to-pressure interaction diagram. Use a three-image strip for airbag placement and form iterations. Place one clean final-prototype image on white. Use a wide treadmill/outdoor pair to show the device in context. Close with a restrained comparison of breathing patterns or a short looping clip of the pneumatic cue.

The paper provides reliable factual structure, but its figures should be treated as source material. Most diagrams and charts should be redrawn in the portfolio’s visual language rather than pasted as paper screenshots.

## Draft short-form project description

Breatho is a soft wearable that turns a runner’s steps into a breathing rhythm they can feel. The system senses cadence and creates gentle cycles of pressure around the abdomen, using inflation and release to suggest when to inhale and exhale.

Designed for amateur runners, the project explores how breathing guidance can move away from screens, numbers, and sound toward a cue that is felt directly through the body. Iterative tests shaped the placement and form of the pneumatic feedback, while treadmill and outdoor trials examined whether the rhythm remained understandable during movement.

Early studies suggest that runners can follow the cues without disrupting their stride, supporting more regular breathing patterns and reducing perceived effort. Breatho frames respiration not only as a physiological measurement, but as an embodied skill that interactive wearables can help people perceive and learn.

Role: research framing, wearable design, pneumatic prototyping, interaction design, system development, user testing, and visual communication.
